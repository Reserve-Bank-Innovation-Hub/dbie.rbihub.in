01

What a story is

In shortOne narrative per story, readable by anyone and useful to an economist in the same pass. We draw inferences from the data and stop there. Each story is frozen at a data release. Readers get to play with a few charts where it earns its place, not with every chart.

  • A narrative, not a dashboardEach story has one question, a turn and an ending. Charts arrive in the order the argument needs them. A chart that does not move the argument goes to the economists' drawer or is cut.
  • Two readers, one passThe main text is written so a school teacher follows it and an economist finds nothing loose in it. Every number names its measure in the sentence ("after inflation, against 40 trading partners"). The economist's extras sit beside the text, not in a separate version.
  • Inference, not commentaryWe state what the numbers show and what follows arithmetically from them. We attribute other people's views by name and date. We do not grade policy, predict, or use loaded words such as "distress" or "crisis".
  • Frozen at a vintageA story says what the data said on a named date. It never updates itself. When the numbers move enough to change the story, we publish a new edition and keep the old one, with links both ways.
  • Play where it earns itTwo or three charts per story let readers change the geography, the period or the measure and watch the picture change. The rest are fixed, annotated and readable at a glance.
  • Built from the house vocabularyThe four live stories already share a language: dots re-forming scene by scene, countable units (₹1 of every ₹100), guess-then-reveal, tile maps, pyramids, dumbbells and the export-arc map. New stories reuse it and add one new signature visual each.

Where inference stops

We will say

  • Arithmetic consequences: "wages rose 1.9 times and prices 1.7 times, so a day's wage buys about 12% more".
  • Decompositions: "of gold's rise in rupees, X came from the rupee's fall".
  • Comparisons across time, place and group, with the same measure.
  • "Consistent with" or "coincides with", when timing lines up.
  • Other people's views, quoted, named and dated, when they help the reader.

We will not say

  • Causal claims the data cannot identify ("UPI caused cash to…").
  • Judgements on policy, institutions or governments.
  • Forecasts, targets or "fair value" claims.
  • Loaded words: distress, crisis, failure, success, boom.
  • Anything about RBI's intent. What RBI did, dated, is a fact; why is RBI's to say.
02

Getting it right

In shortWe are a public institution and people will quote us. Every number passes four gates: a claim register recomputed by script, an independent recompute from the DBIE portal itself, a subject review of definitions and breaks, and a named editorial sign-off. Stories are frozen at a release and carry a corrections log.

Claim register

Every sentence with a number gets an ID, its DBIE series code, the transformation (deflator, base, ratio) and the release. A script recomputes every claim from the frozen snapshot and fails the build on any mismatch.

Independent recompute

A second person recomputes every claim from the DBIE portal or the RBI publication, not from our mirror. This catches mirror bugs. One is already known: our bulletin LRS file has its columns mislabelled.

Subject review

Definitions, base years, breaks, revisions and the latest official vintage, checked against RBI's Bulletin, Annual Report and Handbook. For contested topics, a referee panel tries to refute each claim, as the ₹2 lakh line review did.

Editorial sign-off

Plain-language edit, the neutrality list above, the chart checklist below, and a named, dated sign-off recorded in the story's own record.

Freezing and editions

  • Vintage stamp. Every story shows "Data as of DBIE release version, retrieved DD-MM-YYYY", and every chart's drawer names its series and release. The numbers are compiled into the story at build time, as the live stories do with data.gen.ts. Nothing is fetched live.
  • Editions, not edits. When new data would change a headline, we publish a new edition at its own address (for example /stories/cash-didnt-die/2027) and keep the old one, linked both ways. St Louis Fed's ALFRED keeps every data vintage in the same spirit ALFRED, and Our World in Data separates live from archived charts OWID.
  • Corrections, separated from revisions. Following the ONS model, a revision is new data and a correction is our error ONS. Corrections are dated and shown at the top of the story; presentation changes go in a footer log.
  • Review date. Each story gets a review date (suggested: twelve months). On that date someone checks whether the story still holds and decides between "still holds", "new edition" or "archive note".

Chart checklist for gate 4

  • Scales and windows

    Bars start at zero. Dual axes only with a reason. The claim survives moving the start and end dates; no window begins at a trough or peak, such as the pandemic year.

  • Real, nominal and bases

    Deflator and base year named. No splicing across a base change without an official linking factor. GDP ratios on one GDP base only.

  • Breaks and composition

    Definition changes, category splits and mergers marked on the chart, such as the HDFC merger in July 2023 and the payments redefinition in November 2019.

  • Provisional and revised

    The latest points flagged if provisional. One vintage quoted per number, and named.

  • Units and framing

    Crore and lakh consistent, ₹ and US$ never mixed in one axis, stock never compared with flow, percentage direction stated (a 92% rise in rupees per dollar is a 48% fall in the rupee's dollar value).

  • Access and honesty

    Colour-blind-safe, table fallback, alt text, mobile legibility, no more decimals than the data supports, no causal verbs in captions.

    • Why this matters alreadyThe first-pass numbers in our chat discussion, all correctly computed from DBIE, would still have misled in five places. The 11.7% cash-to-GDP ratio uses the retired 2011-12 GDP base. The household ratios do too, and on the new base the story's ending changes. The wage comparison runs into the June 2025 rebase of the farm-labourer price index. "Private transfers" includes more than migrants' remittances. The bond-ownership table splits a category in March 2022 and absorbs a merger in September 2023. None of these is a coding error; each is a definition the reader would never see.

    04

    One lens across every story: what ₹1 lakh buys

    In shortMoney means little until you can hold it. In 2001 ₹1 lakh bought about 224 grams of gold, enough for a heavy necklace set. In 2011 it bought 42 grams, about a pair of bangles; in 2021, 21 grams, about a chain; in 2026, under 7 grams, about a ring. The same ₹1 lakh can be shown as a mason's working days, US dollars, quintals of wheat at the support price, or its worth in today's prices. That translation becomes one shared visual device across all the stories, and the flagship story S0.

Our reading The lens turns every index into something a reader can picture without losing the arithmetic.

    Rules for using the lens honestly

  • Only real rupee prices

    DBIE carries actual rupee prices for a short list: gold, silver, wages by occupation and state, support prices by crop, exchange rates, per-capita income by state, interest rates, small-savings rates. Most other prices are indices, which tell you how much a price changed, not what it was. For those we say "₹1 lakh of 2001 food spending would cost ₹X today", never "₹1 lakh bought Y kg of onions".

  • Say what the price is

    The Mumbai bullion price is not what a jeweller charges: no making charges, no GST, and the 2024 and 2026 import-duty changes sit inside it. Wages are daily rates for one occupation in rural India. Support prices are what the government offers, not what every farmer gets.

  • Objects are a guide, not a measure

    "About a pair of bangles" helps a reader; the number beside it is the claim. The gram thresholds behind each object name need a cited source, or the reader chooses the object themselves.

  • One yardstick, named

    Real values use one price index throughout, named, with its base links shown. CPI for industrial workers has the longest unbroken chain on DBIE. The farm-labourer index suits rural stories (S6, S10). Decision which yardstick is the house default.

    S1

    Two numbers, one rupee

    Wave 1The rupee and the worldEffort: large

    The rupee went from ₹46 to ₹88 a dollar in 22 years and made headlines at every step. Measured against 40 trading partners and adjusted for prices, it ended within a point of where it started. Both numbers are true; they answer different questions.

    ₹45.95 → ₹88.31
    rupees per US dollar, yearly average, 2003-04 to 2025-26 exchange rates FY
    −44%
    nominal effective rate (40 currencies, trade weights), Apr 2004 → Jul 2026: 138.5 → 78.0 REER/NEER monthly
    90.9 → 91.8
    real effective rate over the same months, base 2015-16 = 100 REER/NEER monthly
    ≈108 → ≈91
    real rate, Nov 2024 peak to mid-2026: a bigger swing in 20 months than across 22 years Drishti, 04-08-2026

    Narrative

    1. Guess. "Since 2004 the rupee has lost about half its value against the dollar. How much less competitive are Indian goods abroad?" A slider from 0 to 60%.
    2. The fall everyone sees. The dollar line, and the 40-currency nominal index, falling 44%.
    3. The turn. Prices rose faster in India than in its partners. Adjust for that and the real index is flat: 90.9 then, 91.8 now.
    4. The swings that do matter. The real rate climbs to about 108 in late 2024, then drops to about 89–92 by 2026. Short swings, not the long slide, move competitiveness.
    5. "Record low" and "undervalued" can both be true. 2026 is the example. Close with what each number answers.

    For the reader

    • Why a falling rupee is mostly a story about prices, not about the economy shrinking.
    • What the headline "record low" does and does not tell you.
    • A personal hook: what a $1,000 course fee cost in 2004 and today, and in "real" terms.

    For economists

    • The decomposition, live: real change ≈ nominal change + India's inflation − partners' inflation, by year.
    • Toggles: 6- versus 40-currency basket; trade versus export weights; monthly versus annual average.
    • The base issue stated plainly: 100 means parity with 2015-16, not equilibrium.
    • Series codes, the January 2021 rebasing note, and CSVs.

    Fact-check traps 7 items
    • RBI rebased in January 2021: 2004-05 → 2015-16 base, 36 → 40 currencies, back-cast to 2004-05 only RBI Bulletin. Do not splice to the 1993 series.
    • REER of 100 is parity with 2015-16, not "fair value". Never write "overvalued by X%" without "relative to 2015-16".
    • Current REER is CPI-based; older debates used WPI-based measures.
    • Recent months are provisional; outlets quote 108.03 to 109.86 for the same peak. Pin the vintage.
    • Percent direction: ₹45.95 → ₹88.31 is a 92% rise in rupees per dollar and a 48% fall in the rupee's dollar value. Pick one convention.
    • FY2025-26 average is ₹88.31; the 2026 spot rate went past 96. Say which.
    • "Crawl-like", not "crawling peg", and the IMF says the label is statistical and backward-looking.
    S2

    Cash didn't die

    Wave 1Money and paymentsEffort: large

    UPI payments grew nineteen-fold in under seven years and ATM withdrawals fell by a third. Cash in circulation still rose to ₹41.7 lakh crore. People changed how they pay. They did not change how much cash they keep.

    122 → 2,366crore
    UPI payments a month, Nov 2019 → Jul 2026 payment system indicators
    62.7 → 40.8crore
    ATM cash withdrawals a month, same months payment system indicators
    3.4 → 9.7crore
    micro-ATM withdrawals a month (village banking agents) payment system indicators
    ₹16.6 → ₹41.7lakh cr
    currency in circulation, Mar 2016 → Mar 2026 reserve money components

    Narrative

    1. Guess. "UPI now handles over 2,000 crore payments a month. Since 2016, has the cash people hold gone up or down?"
    2. The ebb. November 2016: ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes, 86% of the value of cash, withdrawn. Cash falls to 8.7% of GDP.
    3. The rain. UPI arrives and multiplies. Debit-card payments fall from 41.8 to 10.2 crore a month: UPI's first replacement was the card, not the note.
    4. The tide returns. Cash held climbs back to around its pre-2016 share. ATM withdrawals fall. People hold notes and spend them less.
    5. Where cash still moves. Micro-ATMs at village banking agents nearly triple. Close on the stock-versus-flow distinction, the core of the story.

    For the reader

    • The answer to "if everyone uses UPI, why is there more cash?": paying and keeping are different jobs.
    • What happened to the ₹2,000 note, in one scene.
    • A "your monthly cash" comparison: average ATM withdrawal rose from about ₹4,500 to about ₹5,400.

    For economists

    • Transaction demand against store-of-value and precautionary demand, as in RBI's own research RBI Occasional Paper RBI Bulletin, 24-09-2025.
    • Toggles: CIC or currency with the public; GDP base; March or average-of-year.
    • The global cash paradox for context ECB 2021 BIS 2018.

    The 3D layer

    In shortSujan approved three.js for the stories on 08-10-2026. In our stories, 3D sets the scene and moves the reader between ideas; it never carries a number. Whenever a reader is meant to read a value, the camera settles into a flat, front-on view, and the values there match the 2D chart exactly. S1, S2 and S3 are the first three stories to use it.

    The rule: 3D stages, flat reads

    1. No value is carried by depth. There are no 3D bars, no 3D pies and no extruded maps that carry values. Perspective makes near things look bigger, and people will quote our charts.
    2. Every reading moment is flat. The camera settles into an orthographic, front-on view, in which heights are linear and exact. Labels are HTML overlays drawn from the claim register, not text baked into the 3D scene.
    3. One source of numbers. Scenes read the same frozen data.gen.ts as the 2D charts. There is no separate data path to check.
    4. A still fallback for every scene. Readers who have turned on reduced motion, who have no WebGL, who are on a low-end phone, or who print the page all get the flat chart instead. Screen readers get the data table.
    5. Motion follows the scroll and runs both ways. Scrolling back reverses each scene. Nothing the reader needs to know sits inside an autoplaying loop.
    6. Gate 4 adds one check. Before sign-off, the flat frame of each scene is compared with its 2D chart, value by value.

    Beyond stories: a product in itself

    In shortThe research turned up dozens of plain questions people keep asking, each answerable from DBIE with one chart and one paragraph. Together with the stories, the ₹1 lakh lens and a kit for economists, that is a product: the public's reference for Indian economic data, from the institution that hosts the data.

  • DBIE Answers

    Short, frozen, dated answers to the questions in section 03: "Why is there more cash than ever?", "Who owns India's debt?", "Is the rupee undervalued?". Each is one chart, one paragraph, the series code and a link to the full story. In the StatsCAN Plus mould: under three minutes. Checked through the same gates, but faster, because each has one or two claims.

  • The ₹1 lakh calculator

    The lens as a standing tool: any amount, any year, any yardstick. It is the most shareable thing on the site and the obvious entry point from social media. Every output card carries its sources and vintage.

  • The economist's kit

    Per chart: CSV, the transformation script, the DBIE series codes, and a ready query against the data API that is already in this repository. Per story: a notebook that reproduces every number. This is what turns citations into trust.

  • Chart of the month

    One chart, one paragraph, released with a new DBIE release. Keeps the site alive between long stories without breaking the frozen rule: each one is dated and never edited.

  • Embeds for journalists

    Frozen, dated embeds of any chart with a "cite this" line. OWID's archived embeds are the model. People will quote us anyway; this makes them quote the right number and vintage.

  • Ask the data, carefully

    The repository already has an MCP package over the database. A guarded "ask DBIE" that answers only from series and shows its query could come later. It needs its own fact-check design, so it is a later phase.

    Open questions

    In shortDecisions that change what we build. Each has a suggested answer to react to. The ones on governance and sign-off matter most, because they decide how fast anything can ship.

    Governance and accuracy

    1. Who signs off a story?Gate 4 needs a name. Options: an RBIH editor, an RBI economist (DEPR or DSIM), an external referee, or a combination.an RBIH editor signs; an RBI DSIM or DEPR reviewer checks definitions for stories that touch RBI's own data or operations (S1, S2, S5).
    2. Does any story need RBI clearance?S1 (rupee), S2 (currency) and S5 (RBI's bond holdings) describe RBI's own operations, even neutrally.share drafts of those three with the relevant RBI department before publication, as a courtesy review, not a clearance.
    3. Byline and disclaimer?House view or authors' view changes how quotes are attributed. BoE and RBI's Mint Street Memos use an authors'-views disclaimer."RBIH data team" byline with a line that the analysis is RBIH's and not RBI's.
    4. Only DBIE numbers, or other official sources too?S4 needs RBI Annual Report tables; S3 benefits from RBI's remittance survey; S8 from EAC-PM.headline numbers from DBIE or another RBI publication only; other sources only as attributed context.
    5. Wait for new bases, or publish on old ones?S2, S4 and S8 all hinge on base years in transition.S2 on the new GDP base once the back series lands; S4 waits; S8 publishes on the 2011-12 base, frozen and labelled.
    6. How public is the corrections log?on the story page, dated, as ONS does.
    7. Who owns the review date on each story?the story's author, with a 12-month default.

    Audience and voice

    1. Is the voice the same as the live stories ("A So What? data story")?yes, with the drawer adding the economist's layer.
    2. Hindi, and other languages?The lens and the jewellery framing travel well; translation doubles the copy and the checks.S0 in Hindi from launch; others later.
    3. Mobile first?Most Indian traffic is mobile. The constellation and the border need different layouts on a phone.design every signature visual for 390 px first.
    4. How far can attributed views go?Quoting economists helps readers, but a quote can read as endorsement.quote institutions freely; quote individuals only on both sides of a debate, never as the story's conclusion.
    5. The jewellery objects: our weights, or the reader's choice?reader chooses the object; we show grams.

    Product and scope

    1. Which yardstick is the house default for "in today's prices"?CPI for industrial workers for long series; CPI combined from 2011 where the story allows.
    2. Is DBIE Answers in scope now, or after wave 1?after S0, because S0's calculator is its first answer.
    3. Do we publish code and notebooks publicly?yes, per story, in a public repository.
    4. Launch cadence?one story every three to four weeks; Answers weekly once the shell exists.
    5. Edition addresses?/stories/<slug> always shows the latest edition with a banner listing older ones at /stories/<slug>/<year>.
    6. Any topics off-limits?Monetary-policy effectiveness, RBI's balance sheet and inflation targeting are all in DBIE.off-limits for now; revisit after wave 1.

    Section headings and the existing four

    1. Adopt the five headings?The rupee and the world; Money and payments; Households; Government and debt; States.yes, and move the two external-debt stories under the first and fourth.
    2. Retrofit the four live stories to the new shell and gates?yes, as phase 0's test case, starting with the ₹2 lakh line.
    3. Should the live stories carry a vintage stamp now?yes, immediately: it costs little and protects us.
    4. Who reviews this plan before we start?you, then the data team; version 1.1 records the decisions.
