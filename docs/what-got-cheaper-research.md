# What got cheaper: what the research says

The story "What got cheaper" reads the Office of the Economic Adviser's wholesale price index on DBIE, the 2011-12 series for every commodity code from April 2012 to April 2026, and claims that what cost ₹100 at wholesale in 2011-12 cost ₹156 in 2025-26 while the typical good stood at ₹144 (C1); that 33 of the 697 goods cost less in rupees than in 2011-12, led by solar power systems at ₹46 (C2); that 103 goods doubled, 59 of them grown, reared or mined, led by jasmine at ₹596 (C3); that the typical grown good stands at ₹201 against ₹139 for the typical factory-made good, and that over four linked series since 1982-83 grown goods rose about twice as much as factory goods (C4); that consumer prices rose 77% between 2013 and 2025 against 40% for the wholesale basket, with services and goods rising at about the same pace (C5); and that measured in a rural labourer's days of work 623 of 697 goods got cheaper between 2014-15 and 2024-25, while against the prices rural labourers pay the day buys about what it did (C6). This review tested those claims and six blind spots (goods only, at the factory gate, B1; no quality adjustment and quotations carried forward, B2; the index's gold item against the price of gold, B3; weights fixed in 2011-12, B4; seasonal goods, provisional months, placeholder rows and the end of the series, B5; bases joined only at major-group level, B6). Four reading passes catalogued about 180 sources, the world literature first and then Indian work, each opened where it could be; three referees then tried to refute each claim from the evidence, the measurement and the alternative explanations, re-deriving the story's numbers from the repository's mirror of the same DBIE tables without the story's processor; and a critic read every sentence of the draft against its sources, the data and the editor's style rules. Figures the referees computed are reported as the panel's findings. The review was completed on 07-10-2026, with data to April 2026.

## How the review was done

Four themes were read in a world pass and an Indian pass: the best published stories and charts on prices diverging and how they are presented (BEST); why the prices of different goods diverge (WHY); how the wholesale price index is made and what its numbers can say (MET); and how the editor wants prose written, recovered from the editor's own edits to the previous story (STYLE). Source ids follow the theme. The first referee re-derived every item-level claim from the DBIE CSV files without the processor and tested the window, the treatment of seasonal zeros, the Office's final figures, the stale-quotation question and the bullion comparison; the second re-derived the long series, the consumer index and the wage claims, tested the join year, the window and the yardstick, and pulled the American series from the Bureau of Labor Statistics; the critic rendered every sentence with its live figures and checked each against the style guide, the cited source and the arithmetic. A change was required where the wording was wrong or misleading in the light of the evidence; every proposed figure is produced by the story's processor from the DBIE files, except figures cited from other publications, which are named where they appear. Limits: the Office's final item-level figures for March and April 2026 are not on DBIE, so the page keeps DBIE's provisional ones and says so; the Office's quotation counts come from the 2017 manual's annex, matched by weight; the Office could not be asked what its gold item prices or why some quotations stood still for years; MoSPI's 2012 item list was not opened, so the share of medicines in "health" is unverified; the web-search allowance ran out in the Indian literature pass, so Drèze and Himanshu on real wages and an Indian household-level study of inflation inequality were not found.

## C1 ₹100 of goods in 2011-12 cost ₹156 in 2025-26

The all-commodities index averaged 155.96 over April 2025 to March 2026 on DBIE and 155.99 on the Office's final figures; the rise of 56% over fourteen years is 3.23% a year. The median good is 144.35, the weighted median 145.9 and the mean 157.1; 366 goods sit between ₹100 and ₹150. The draft's explanation of the gap between ₹156 and ₹144 was refuted: milk (₹4.44 of every ₹100 of weight) stands at ₹191, the 82nd percentile; diesel (₹3.10) at ₹161; only electricity (₹3.06) near the middle. The ten heaviest goods average ₹162 and supply 12 of the 56 points; the 103 goods that doubled weigh ₹13.4 but supply 18 points; the 33 that fell subtract 0.3; 436 goods carrying ₹63 of weight rose less than the basket. By major group the rise is 20.5 points from primary articles (on ₹22.6 of weight), 6.0 from fuel and power and 29.5 from manufactured products. The page now says the basket stands ₹12 above the typical good because the goods that rose most rose much further than any fell, and that an average of the goods counted equally gives ₹157. April 2026 alone gives ₹167 because fuel and power jumped 18% in the month; the page names it as the last month of the series and as provisional.

## C2 33 goods cost less in rupees than in 2011-12

The count is 33 over the financial year, over the last twelve months and over 2024-25; 26 in April 2026 alone; 32 on the Office's final March figure (hydrogen peroxide moves from 99.2 to 100.2). Twenty-eight are manufactured products and together they weigh ₹2.58. The first referee's stale test, which the processor now computes for every good, finds 12 of the 33 unchanged from the month before in half or more of their months or at one value for two years or more (earth-moving machinery, tanning materials, steam turbines, stainless steel razors, electrical ballasts, computer peripherals, sweet potato, non-coking coal of the highest grade, modems, rubber tubes, wooden boards and windmill turbines); five more within 5% of 2011-12; and 16 that fell by more than 5% in prices that moved most months, all factory-made, weighing ₹1.22. Stale series are 36% of the cheaper goods against 5% to 9% of the other bands, and the cheaper goods rest on fewer quotations (a median of five against ten). The page's cheaper card now carries this, and the story's named fallers are among the sixteen: solar power systems (seven quotations, moving in many steps from ₹73 in April 2012), blankets (eight), colour TVs (six) and anti-cancer drugs (two quotations, a 32% fall in February 2018 mostly reversed by 2019 and a steady fall since; the page says so). Personal computers (unchanged for 101 months), laptops (71) and earth-moving machinery (two steps and 28 months unchanged) were taken out of the story's sentences during the review.

## C3 103 goods cost at least twice what they did

The count is 103 over the year, the last twelve months and the final months only, 91 in 2024-25 and 127 in April 2026; 59 are primary articles, 15 have tripled, and the 103 weigh ₹13.44. The named risers are exact: jasmine ₹596 (four quotations, from ₹385 to ₹979 within the year), coconuts ₹389 (ten quotations, ₹237 in 2023-24 and ₹282 in 2024-25), tomatoes ₹309 (seventeen quotations, ₹150 to ₹475 within the year), silver ₹298 (two quotations that follow the Mumbai price), wheat ₹211 (forty quotations, the most robust number in the paragraph). Zinc concentrate at ₹529 rests on a single captive-mine quotation and stands 3.4 times the index's own zinc metal at ₹156; the page says so and offers the silver by-product in the ore as a likely cause, not a measured one. Nine of the 103 are stale-looking, among them sugarcane and lignite, which are administered prices.

## C4 Grown against made, now and since 1982

Fuel and power's median is ₹143, not the ₹144 of the draft, which took the upper of two middle values; the processor now averages them. The typical grown or mined good costs ₹201 and the typical factory-made good ₹139, while the groups at their weights stand at ₹190, ₹146 and ₹146, which the card now gives beside the medians. The linking factors reproduce the Office's published ones to the third decimal (1.561, 1.873, 2.478 for all commodities; 2.003, 1.690, 1.395 and 1.881, 2.802, 1.663 for the groups), and the 1981-82 group factors match the Reserve Bank's Handbook averages. From unrounded yearly averages the multiples are 10.77, 16.87, 17.00 and 7.95; joined at other years the series both ran they range from 10.5 to 11.4, 15.1 to 17.1, 15.0 to 22.1 and 7.8 to 8.5, so the page says "about 11", "about 17" and "about 8" and leads with the ratio of 2.1, which holds under every join. Two sentences were refuted: factory goods rose as much as the basket from 1982-83 to 1992-93 (×2.18 against ×2.18) and more in the 1980s, and the last year they kept pace was 1995-96; about 65% of the grown-against-made gap had opened by 2011-12 and 62% opened between 2005-06 and 2013-14. The page now says so. The rupee fell from ₹9.67 to ₹88.31 a dollar over the same years, nearly as much as the basket rose, and against both groups alike; it is not an explanation of the gap and the page does not offer it.

## C5 Consumer prices rose 77%, wholesale prices 40%

Every group figure reproduces from DBIE to the point, and the gap between the two indices is 34 to 48 points in every window tried. Calendar 2013 was a year of high food inflation, so the group order changes with the window; the page gives the financial-year rise of the general index beside the calendar one. The draft's "services rose faster than goods" held only from calendar 2013 (the mostly-service groups, housing, health and education, +81% against +76% for the mostly-goods groups, weighted by MoSPI's 2012 weights); over financial years or from the 2012 base the goods rose more, and housing, the one group wholly a service, rose less than the general index in all four windows. The page now says services and goods rose at about the same pace, gives health's lead over everything (12 points) and housing's shortfall (2 points), and notes that education's lead of 2 points disappears over financial years. Gold accounts for about half of personal care's 122%, so the page says "much of it", with the Mumbai gold price's 3.5 times between 2013 and 2025. The American figures the page cites were confirmed from the Bureau of Labor Statistics: hospital and related services +275% and televisions −98% from January 2000 to September 2025, services +50% against goods +20% over 2013–2025. The draft attributed ₹46 and ₹24 to Das and George; those are MoSPI's 2012 weight and the wholesale food index's weight, and the page now cites them so and cites Das and George for their finding that the difference in weights explained about 70% of the gap in 2015-16. Group for group the shop rose faster than the wholesale gate: food 76% against 62% for the wholesale food index.

## C6 In days of work

The non-farm labourer's day averaged ₹237.71 in 2014-15 and ₹396.26 in 2024-25, twelve months in each; the basket rose from 113.9 to 154.9. The count of 623 cheaper and 74 dearer reproduces with unrounded values and barely moves with the broad labouring yardsticks (641, 619, 611 to 614) but falls to 585 from a 2015-16 start, because 2014-15 was the wholesale index's high point before the oil price fell; the method note now gives all of these. Averaging the states' rises equally instead of every village quotation gives 68% rather than 67%. Deflated by the Labour Bureau's own index for rural labourers (802 to 1,311), the non-farm day buys 2% more, which the page prints as "about what it did"; a farm labourer's day against the agricultural labourers' index buys about 9% more, which the page now gives. The count is a statement about wholesale prices, which no labourer pays; at shop prices the general index rose ×1.62 over the window, so the average basket costs 3% fewer days, not 18%. The card's title was changed to "goods cost fewer days' wages at wholesale". The Bureau widened its sample from 600 villages in 20 states to 787 in 34 states and union territories from July 2025, after the last month on DBIE, and warns against comparisons across the change; a future refresh must not simply append.

## B1 Goods only, at the factory gate

The index prices goods at their first sale in bulk, basic prices after discounts and before indirect taxes since the 2011-12 series [MET-2 §2.2; MET-4 Q16; MET-18 Q4]; "WPI basket does not cover services" [MET-4 Q1]. The Reserve Bank's committee noted that "the price quotes of some of the important commodities like milk, LPG and the like are basically taken from retail markets" [MET-26 para II.28], which the method note now says. The RBI dropped the WPI as the policy anchor in 2014 for those reasons and for its revisions [MET-26].

## B2 No quality adjustment; quotations carried forward

None of the Office's documents describes a quality-adjustment method; specifications are fixed and replacements spliced at an overlap, which treats the whole price difference as quality [MET-2 §3.3; MET-17 §2.8.1]; the 2014 terms of reference asked a sub-group to explore a method for "products that undergo rapid and frequent quality differences like computers, mobiles" and none appears [MET-3]. The 2026 methodology note confirms the 2011-12 series filled missing prices by carrying the last reported price forward [MET-16 §8.1]. The page's TV sentence was rewritten so that ₹89 is read as the price of each quoted model over its life, not as the price of bigger sets, and the American −98% as a quality-adjusted index from 1997 [BEST-1]. Sixteen items rest on a single quotation and 92 on three or fewer [MET-2 Annex I].

## B3 The gold item against the price of gold

The Mumbai price of gold on DBIE averaged ₹25,722 per 10 grams over 2011-12 and ₹1,18,421 over 2025-26, 4.60 times; the index's "gold & gold ornaments" item averaged 257.3. Month by month the item moves with gold but by about six-tenths of each proportional move, and its ratio to the rebased Mumbai price drifts from about 0.9 in 2012–2018 to 0.54 in 2026. Silver's item (298.0) follows the Mumbai price (2.84 times) with a correlation of 0.998 in levels. The page prints 4.6 times, says silver "is close to" the Mumbai price and has followed it month by month, and keeps the question of what the gold item prices for the Office, asked once.

## B4 Weights

A weight is the good's share of 2011-12 net traded value, output plus imports less exports [MET-2 §2.3, §2.8; MET-4 Q5], fixed for the life of the series; the page's wording was corrected from "what the country produced". DBIE files one pair the wrong way round, the textile-machinery sub-group as a good and open-end spinning machinery as a sub-group; without the swap the goods' weights add to 100.055. The processor now swaps them back as the Office's file has them and checks every parent's children against its weight. The headline movers are light (solar ₹0.026, jasmine ₹0.031, silver ₹0.031, anti-cancer drugs ₹0.080), but wheat (₹1.03), gold (₹0.97) and colour TVs (₹0.44) are not.

## B5 Seasonal goods, provisional months, placeholders, the end of the series

Six goods carry a 0 in the months they are not compiled, when their weight goes to the rest of the sub-group [MET-2 §2.10]: green peas, cauliflower, mango, orange, grapes and litchi, 505 cells in all; the processor averages them over their priced months, and treating the zeros as prices would add litchi and mango to the cheaper list. DBIE prints the base year, April 2011 to March 2012, as 10,428 rows of exactly 100, which the processor drops; the Office's own file starts in April 2012 [MET-10]. DBIE's March and April 2026 are the provisional figures of the 14 May 2026 release (160.8 and 167.0); the finals are 161.2 and 167.6, and 315 goods were revised in March [MET-11; the Office's final file]. The 2011-12 series ended with April 2026; the Office released a 2022-23 series with 957 goods on 15 June 2026 and will publish the wholesale index for five more years beside producer price indices [MET-12, MET-14].

## B6 Bases joined only at major-group level

The Office links its series by dividing the older one by its average over the next base year and publishes factors only for all commodities and the major groups: "the derivation of linking factors at such granular levels may not yield reliable results" [MET-8, MET-9]. DBIE's monthly table ends the 1981-82 series in December 1999 and begins the 1993-94 series in January 2000, though the Office's files overlap from April 1994 to March 2000 [MET-10]; the processor takes 1994-95 to 1999-00 from the 1993-94 series in DBIE's table of yearly averages, so the chart's join is at 1993-94, where the Office makes it. Each base has its own basket (primary articles weighed ₹32.3 of ₹100 in 1981-82 and ₹22.6 in 2011-12), so the long series compares baskets as well as prices; the page says so.

## Best practice for reading a price index at item level, and how the story measures up

1. Say what the index measures, at which point of sale and with what left out, once and exactly: followed, in the method notes.
2. Compare like periods: a year's average against a year's average, seasonal goods over their priced months: followed.
3. Treat a 0 or a 9999.9 as no index, never as a price: followed.
4. Check the tree: leaves, weights adding to the parent, and the index rebuilt from its groups: followed after the review; the first check was too loose to catch DBIE's mis-filing.
5. Before calling a good cheaper, ask whether its quotation moved: followed after the review; the page now flags the twelve stale-looking goods among the 33.
6. Name the quotation count where a headline rests on one or two: followed for anti-cancer drugs, zinc concentrate and garnet.
7. Do not read a quality-adjusted index and an unadjusted one as the same measure: followed.
8. Join bases the publisher's way, say where the join is, and give the range other joins would give: followed after the review.
9. Round a multiple over four baskets and four decades to "about", and lead with the ratio: followed after the review.
10. Test a "since the 1990s" or "every decade" sentence decade by decade before printing it: not followed in the draft; corrected.
11. Attribute a cause only where a source carries it, and say which candidates are untested: partly followed in the draft (solar's duty, the kerosene subsidy, phones, "fields have less", "one year's shortage" were asserted beyond the sources); corrected.
12. Set a wholesale index against wages and against the deflator the wage's publisher uses, and say which is which: followed after the review.
13. Compute every figure by program with checks, and build the metadata and the listing from the same figures: followed; the listing stays typed.
