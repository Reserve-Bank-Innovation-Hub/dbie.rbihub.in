# The ₹2 lakh line: what the research says

The story "The ₹2 lakh line" reads the Reserve Bank of India's Basic Statistical Returns on DBIE and claims that the growth in bank lending counted by accounts happened below the ₹2 lakh line that defines a small borrowal account (C1); that the smallest loans carry the highest rates (C2); that the count of small accounts peaked in December 2024 and fell by 4.2 crore in eighteen months for two stated reasons (C3); that the fall landed on farm loans in villages, durable loans in big cities and loans to women in the east (C4); and that ten districts hold ₹42 of every ₹100 of bank credit (C5). This review tested those claims and four blind spots (banks only, B1; a line fixed in nominal rupees, B2; accounts rather than people, B3; where a loan is counted, B4). Nineteen reading passes catalogued about 280 sources, the world literature first and then Indian work, each opened where it could be and re-checked against its own page; twenty-three referees then tried to refute each claim from the evidence, the measurement and the alternative explanations, re-deriving the story's numbers from the repository's mirror of the same DBIE tables; a critic read the draft against its sources; and a gap round read the merged bank's own reports and the bank-group tables to size the one entry on which the date of the peak turns. Figures the referees computed from those tables are reported as the panel's findings; the reference list carries the catalogued sources and marks those the panel could not open. The review was completed on 30 September 2026, with data to June 2026. A longer record of the same review, with the full literature discussion, is kept beside this page.

## Corrections after a second check, 1 October 2026

After the panel finished, every figure the page takes from outside publications was opened again, and the story's processor was changed so that the remaining figures come from the data instead of being typed. Where the change table below and the published page differ, the page is the later text.

- **Loans to women.** The processor now adds up every row of quarterly Table 1.10, including districts DBIE prints on two rows. Across the comparable states women held 11.4 crore loan accounts in March 2024 and 9.9 crore in June 2026; Karnataka fell by 20% instead of rising by 5%; 375 of 733 comparable districts lost loans to women. The north-eastern states now compare; Assam is left out because of the December 2024 entry.
- **The December 2024 entry.** The small finance banks' urban small loans rose by 0.69 crore that quarter (Table 2.1) and Assam's loan accounts by 0.71 crore (Table 1.3); by June 2026 Assam's accounts were 0.98 crore above September 2024. The page derives these.
- **Rates.** The Reserve Bank's own rate on credit cards is 37.0% for March 2026 (quarterly Table 1.9). Read the same way from quarterly Table 2.5, March 2014 priced loans up to ₹25,000 at about 12.5% and loans above ₹100 crore at about 11.6%, so the gap has widened. The RBI's 2008 survey of small borrowal accounts (RBI Bulletin, May 2011) gives 11.5% on small accounts against 12.3% on large ones.
- **Concentration.** By place of sanction (Table 2.14) the ten largest districts hold 48% of credit and Mumbai's two districts 21%; by use, 42% and 16%. In 2010 the ten largest held 58% and Mumbai 22%.
- **MFIN.** The body is the Micro Finance Industry Network. Its July 2024 guardrails capped a borrower's microfinance debt at ₹2 lakh with four lenders, effective August 2024; its November 2024 directive cut the lenders to three and counted unsecured retail loans within the ₹2 lakh, effective April 2025. Banks' microfinance book fell 28.5% in the year to June 2026, mostly by reclassification to retail, by MFIN's account.
- **Sa-Dhan.** Its member lenders outside the banks charged a weighted average of 22% to 24% across the quarters of 2025-26, not 19% to 24%; the 19.2% was a 2024-25 portfolio yield.
- **TransUnion CIBIL.** The 28% (March 2026) against 11% (March 2017) is credit-active adults aged 18 to 60, from one bureau. Its women's report counts 16 crore credit-active women in December 2025.
- **CRIF High Mark.** At the end of 2024 it counted 14.6 crore microfinance loans and 8.4 crore borrowers; the 13.9 crore and 7.8 crore in the drafts are Equifax figures published by Sa-Dhan. Consumer durable loans across lenders rose from 10.3 crore to 11.3 crore in the year to June 2026; no December 2024 figure is published.
- **The ₹2 lakh line.** The primary source is the RBI's introduction to the March 1999 Basic Statistical Returns; regional rural banks moved to the same line in March 2002.
- **Gopal and Schnabl (2022).** Total lending grew no less in the counties that depended most on banks; "had not fallen at all" overstated it.
- **Population.** The derived state populations are within 0.5% of the Technical Group on Population Projections' figures for 1 March 2026.

## How the review was done

Eleven themes were read, each in a world pass and an Indian pass: measuring inclusion; why small loans cost more; capital rules and consumer credit; microfinance crises; gender and credit; the spatial concentration of credit; fixed nominal thresholds; substitution to non-bank lenders; the policy timeline from November 2023 to August 2026; what the RBI itself has said; and the papers that read the same BSR series. Source ids follow the theme (INC, PRICE, MACRO, MFI, GENDER, GEO, DEF, SUB, TL, RBI, SD), with item-specific ids for sources the referees added and the prefix GAP1 for the gap round. Each claim was refereed three times and each blind spot twice, always with the brief to refute; the referees re-derived the story's numbers from the DBIE mirror and split them by bank group, band, product, state and quarter. Every figure reproduced except one, set out under C4. The critic's round tested each sentence of the draft against the source it cites; where a figure is the panel's inference rather than a reported number, this revision says so. The gap round read slice Small Finance Bank's annual reports for 2024-25 and 2025-26, its Pillar III disclosure and the press coverage of its merger [GAP1-1 to GAP1-15], and the bank-group tables 2.4 and 3.7 beside Tables 2.1, 1.3 and 1.10 [GAP1-16 to GAP1-20]. A change is required only where the wording is wrong or misleading in the light of the evidence; any proposed figure must be produced by the story's processor. Limits: web-search budgets ran out in several passes; the RBI's document host serves a bot check, so some RBI documents were read from HTML pages, mirrors or transcriptions, marked "(not opened)" in the references; no RBI page opened dates the ₹2 lakh cut-off directly, which rests on Chavan [PRICE-I3] and the Inclusive Finance India reports [SD14]; the merged bank publishes no count of loan accounts or borrowers, so the size of the December 2024 entry is inferred from the step in five DBIE tables, which agree within 2 lakh; the dates on which the individual small finance banks were scheduled and began filing the return are not sourced beyond the mirror's bank-group rows and Wikipedia; the Census 2011 district populations are Wikipedia transcriptions; the RBI's press releases on the two returns were read but are not in the source list; and no peer-reviewed evaluation of the 2024 to 2026 microfinance retrenchment or of the November 2023 measure exists yet.

## C1 Inclusion happened below ₹2 lakh

**The literature.** Counting accounts below a size line is what the field does: Beck and co-authors made loan accounts per capita the access proxy and paired it with the loan-income ratio for depth [INC-G3]; Čihák and co-authors and Finance for All? keep the extensive and intensive margins apart; India's own series in the IMF Financial Access Survey is loan accounts per 1,000 adults. What the field does not do is call an account a person: the FAS counts "one borrower, irrespective of the number of loan accounts held" and notes that India reports accounts [INC-G15]; Honohan corrects account counts for multiple holding [INC-G5]; Findex puts formal borrowing at 15.1 per cent of adults, about 16 crore people, against 63.3 per cent borrowing from any source [INC-G13]. Indian readers accept the measure but read the long series as a decline: small accounts were 85 per cent of accounts in the 1970s and 77 per cent in 2015, their share of money 23.1 per cent in 1999 and 8.3 in 2015 [PRICE-I3]; Shetty documents the 1990s collapse [SD5]; Sriram asks whether the growth is "greater inclusion or a product of sachetization", private banks holding half the small accounts [SD14]. The bureaus count people: 28 per cent of adults aged 18 to 60 credit-active in March 2026, about 25 crore, up from 11 per cent in 2017 [INC-I16]; 10.7 crore cards for 5.2 crore people [INC-I17]; 10.3 crore microfinance loans for 6.6 crore borrowers [INC-I14]; the 2022 to 2024 microfinance boom was more loans to existing clients [MFI-I14]. Digital NBFCs sanctioned 77 per cent of 2025-26's personal loans by count [SUB-I10].

**Verdict: supported for the count, contested for the framing; high confidence on the count, medium on the framing.** The arithmetic is exact and the measure is the field's own. The contest is over one sentence, and three findings bear on it. The reporting perimeter moved twice. The bank-group rows of quarterly Table 2.1 carry no small finance banks in June 2017, the base quarter, and carry them from September 2017 [GAP1-18]; the six converted microfinance lenders had begun banking between April 2016 and April 2017 (Wikipedia [C1-MEASUREMENT5]; the dates on which each was scheduled and began filing the return were not sourced), and they hold 3.05 crore small accounts by June 2026, a quarter of the net rise, with borrowers who existed before conversion [SD14]. And the December 2024 high point rests on a one-quarter entry of about 0.7 crore tiny accounts at urban small finance bank branches, when the loan book of the fintech Slice, until then at an NBFC outside the return, was amalgamated into North East Small Finance Bank, registered in Guwahati, with effect from 27 October 2024 [GAP1-1]. The step is 0.69 to 0.73 crore in five tables: small finance banks' urban small accounts 67.1 to 136.3 lakh in Table 2.1 [GAP1-18], their accounts of ₹25,000 or less 22.9 to 93.0 lakh in Table 2.4 [GAP1-16], their "other personal loans" 23.4 to 96.4 lakh in Table 3.7 [GAP1-17], Assam's accounts 47.5 to 118.9 lakh in Table 1.3 [GAP1-19] and Kamrup Metropolitan's 12.6 to 84.1 lakh in Table 1.10 [GAP1-20]. The bank publishes no count of loan accounts or borrowers [GAP1-1, GAP1-2], so the size is the panel's inference from those steps, not a reported figure; net of it the count peaked in March 2024. Credit cards of ₹2 lakh or less rose from 2.15 to 7.32 crore, 19 to 32 per cent of small accounts, 43 per cent of the rise; accounts above the line grew 3.3 times against 2.1 below, and the small share of accounts fell from 73 to 63 per cent. The RBI's own inclusion index rose to 70.0 for March 2026 in the year the count fell, and its press releases on these returns report growth in rupees, not in accounts (the panel's reading of the releases, which are not in the source list). The ₹6 is the end of a long slide and a stock share; small loans turn over in months, so their share of a year's lending is larger [MACRO-I11].

**Change.** The so-what must say this is the part of inclusion that happened at banks, counted in accounts; name the small finance bank and card shares of the rise; note that loans above the line grew faster and that most of the smallest loans are now made outside the returns; and give ₹6 as a stock share at the end of a decline. seriesStart and seriesPeak must name the two perimeter entries, keeping December 2024 as the published high point and saying what it contains; the closing keeps "since the end of 2024" and says the count is of bank loan accounts; the accounts scene must not say "loans in the country".

## C2 Small loans cost the most

**The literature.** Rates fall with loan size everywhere because each loan carries a fixed cost: Rosenberg and co-authors decompose microcredit interest, profit under a tenth [PRICE-G1]; Cull, Demirgüç-Kunt and Morduch put unit cost at 18 per cent of portfolio for the smallest-loan lenders against 11 for banks, and the lenders charging most are not the most profitable [PRICE-G4]; Stiglitz and Weiss explain rationing of the small end [PRICE-G5]; rate caps cut loans to risky borrowers, not their price; a banded rate is not the price paid [PRICE-G1]. In India the only account-level test finds the size-rate relation only above ₹2 lakh; the RBI's own surveys of small borrowal accounts are reported, in a thesis the panel could not open, to have found small accounts paying less than large ones in every round to 2008, durables and personal loans excepted [PRICE-I4, not opened; transcription]; the ceiling on small-account pricing went in 2010 [PRICE-I3]; Gupta read Table 2.1 for 2023 to the story's conclusion [GENDER-I5]. Outside banks the same loans cost 19.18 per cent (Sa-Dhan's 2024-25 yield, on funds at 11.50 and operating cost of 7.09 [PRICE-I9]), 24.10 per cent in the March 2026 quarter [SUB-I17], 23.5 to 24.5 in NCAER's survey, 36 to 49 on cards with no ceiling [PRICE-I7]; NBFC-MFIs lost money in 2024-25; crop loans are at 7 per cent, 4 on prompt repayment [PRICE-I14].

**Verdict: supported with caveats, high confidence.** No artefact could manufacture the gradient and every one made it steeper. In the ≤₹25,000 band 64.3 per cent of the money sits in the open cell "13% and above", which the story values at 14; its processor calls the result "an indication of the slope, not a measured average" [C2-MEASUREMENT7]. The RBI's quarterly Table 1.9 prices credit cards at 37.01 per cent in March 2026; with cards at that rate the band reads about 16.7, with the range at 20 it reads 16.3, and the pre-2021 table with ranges to "20% and above" gives 16.3 to 20.8, while the band above ₹100 crore stays at 7.6. The December 2024 entry does not touch this: the added accounts sit in the smallest band, and their money, about ₹1,500 crore, is 0.13 per cent of small borrowal outstanding [GAP1-16]. Four caveats: the band is 74 per cent personal-loan accounts and a third cards held two to a person [INC-I17], so "those who borrow the least" is not what the table measures; the return records the contracted rate before fees, cards at their finance-charge rate and crop loans at 7 though prompt repayers pay 4; the gap is new (March 2014: 11.35 per cent up to ₹25,000 against 11.55 above ₹100 crore) and the big-loan share under 8 per cent was 32.8 in March 2024 and 63.2 in March 2026 after the 2025 easing; and it holds inside banks, where the price is the cheapest formal one these borrowers see.

**Change.** "About 12.4% on average" becomes "at least 12.4% on average, and in truth more", with the open range and the RBI's 37 per cent for cards; the so-what's verdict becomes a statement about the smallest loans, the products in them, the newness of the gap and the price outside banks; the figure's units and note say the small-band rates are lower bounds before fees; priceAccounts says each card is counted at its contract rate.

## C3 Small loans peaked in December 2024 and have fallen since, for stated reasons

**The literature.** Capital rules cut lending at the banks and loans they touch, fast and by reallocation, with a third leaking to unregulated lenders; effects are weaker in busts. Microfinance crises follow fast growth and multiple borrowing, take two to four years to bottom, and have funding squeezes and politics among their causes [MFI-G6]. A fixed nominal line misdated the US small-business recovery by two to four years [DEF-G5]; the test is the below-line count against all loans [SD5]. The RBI's record: the November 2023 circular raised the risk weight on unsecured consumer credit to 125 per cent and on credit-card receivables to 150, excluded housing, education, vehicle and gold loans and never covered agriculture [MACRO-G16]; the moderation was judged satisfactory by February 2025; the weights on cards and personal loans stayed, while banks' microfinance loans went back to 100 per cent on 25 February 2025 and the add-on on bank lending to NBFCs came off on 1 April 2025 [MFI-I8]; the December 2025 FSR attributes the unsecured-retail fall to the measure and records microfinance credit down for six quarters [TL18]; agriculture credit grew 15.7 per cent in 2025-26 [RBI18]. Microfinance: new loans down 29 per cent and PAR 31-180 from 2.0 to 6.4 per cent by December 2024 [MFI-I1]; guardrails of ₹2 lakh per borrower and four lenders from 8 July 2024 [TL4], three lenders and unsecured retail counted from January and April 2025 [TL6], expected to affect over 20 per cent of borrowers; a funding winter and a 5 per cent shift of the book to retail bureaus by banks; seven quarters of contraction, banks down 30 per cent against 2.7 for NBFC-MFIs; state laws in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu [MFI-I11]; loans down from 13.2 to 10.3 crore and borrowers from 8.0 to 6.6 crore in the year to June 2026 [INC-I14]; a turn "driven primarily by NBFC-MFIs and higher loan sizes" [TL22]. The merged bank's own record: the scheme amalgamating Slice's NBFC, Garagepreneurs Internet, into North East Small Finance Bank took effect on 27 October 2024, and the bank's net loan portfolio rose from ₹802 crore to ₹2,905 crore in 2024-25 "due to the amalgamation" [GAP1-1]; the bank's gross advances rose 53 per cent in 2025-26, 79 per cent of them unsecured, and 55 per cent in the year to June 2026 [GAP1-2, GAP1-10]; its chief executive describes a shift to secured lending as prospective, from 4 per cent of the book at the merger to 22 in April 2026 [GAP1-9].

**Verdict: supported with caveats on the fall, contested on what the peak contains and on the reach of the first cause; medium confidence, two of the three referees having given medium.** The published series does peak in December 2024, at 27.28 crore against 27.22 in March 2024, and the story's 4.2 crore is what that series shows. But the count had stopped growing in March 2024, was 27.18 in June and 26.85 in September, and December's rise rests on a step of about 0.7 crore in small finance banks' urban accounts of ₹25,000 or less while every other group and band fell [GAP1-18, GAP1-16]: the loan book of the fintech Slice, amalgamated into North East Small Finance Bank with effect from 27 October 2024 [GAP1-1, GAP1-5, GAP1-6]. Net of the entry, December 2024 reads 26.55 to 26.59 crore, below March, June and September 2024, and the count peaked in March 2024; December quarters were historically the strongest, so seasonality cannot make a December peak. The book did not run off: the step stands 0.87 to 1.11 crore above its September 2024 base by June 2026 [GAP1-16, GAP1-17], in line with the bank's advances growing 53 per cent in 2025-26 and 55 per cent in the year to June 2026, still about four-fifths unsecured [GAP1-2, GAP1-9, GAP1-10]. So the entry does not shrink the fall; it hides part of it: net of the book, small accounts fell 4.4 to 4.6 crore from December 2024 and 5.0 to 5.2 crore from March 2024 (an earlier panel estimate of 4.8 crore assumed the book stayed at 0.7 crore). The share statements survive (72.9 per cent of accounts in March 2024 and 70.6 per cent net of the entry in December 2024 are both "more than seven in every ten"), and the money share is untouched, the book being at most 0.4 per cent of small borrowal outstanding [GAP1-18]. The story's processor computes no series net of the entry, so any such figure must come from a new step (Table 2.4, small finance banks' ≤₹25,000 band, base September 2024) rather than from copy. The first cause is real for the loans it covered and should be dated and narrowed, not discounted: the 125 per cent weight on unsecured personal loans and 150 on cards stood through the window [MACRO-G16], and metropolitan small accounts peaked in December 2023, the month after the circular, at 10.39 crore, falling to 9.98 by December 2024 and 9.47 by June 2025 [GAP1-18]. But it never applied to farm loans, which are the bulk of the fall; banks' microfinance loans were taken back out of it in February 2025 [MFI-I8]; and cards below the line still grew in number (6.87 to 7.32 crore), so the rule slowed new lending rather than closing accounts. What fell was agriculture-coded accounts (9.38 to 6.66 crore), trade, industry and durables, while bank personal loans grew 16 per cent by value and durable lending across all lenders 36.6 per cent [INC-I15]. The second cause is inside the return: 3.30 of the 4.19 crore is at private banks, at microfinance-sized balances, and banks cut their microfinance books hardest; the mechanism was guardrails set at the BSR's own ₹2 lakh line, a funding squeeze, state laws and write-offs (PAR 180+ with write-offs 10.7 to 17.1 per cent [SUB-I17]). Drift is bounded: accounts above the line grew 2.24 crore in the eighteen months before the peak and 2.34 after; the ≤₹25,000 band lost 1.37 crore, which prices up 5.7 per cent [DEF-I12] could not carry over; loans of every size fell 1.85 crore. The June 2026 quarter's fall is mostly 0.79 crore metropolitan durable accounts leaving with balances rising.

**Change.** seriesPeak keeps December 2024 and says what the count contains and that it had stopped growing in March 2024; seriesFall gives the bound, says the fall is at banks and that it is if anything larger among the loans that were there before the entry; where-they-went dates the rule to the month metropolitan small loans turned, narrows it to cards and personal loans and says it never reached farm loans, and gives the microfinance retreat its mechanism; the so-what keeps "seven and a half years" and adds that the count barely moved through 2024 and what December contains; the closing keeps "since the end of 2024" and 4 crore. A series net of the entry, if the story wants one, needs a new processor step.

## C4 Who lost: farm loans in villages, durables in big cities, women in the east

**The literature.** Retrenchments drop weaker borrowers first, women among them, agriculture first, consumer-durable lenders first; but gender gaps are established within lender and product, are small when they are, often run through non-application, and microfinance is a women's product by construction, 73 per cent of clients worldwide and over 95 per cent in India [GENDER-G4]. Indian readers of the gender tables use shares and ratios: women 34 per cent of individual borrowers, 49.6 per cent of rural small accounts and 19.8 of metropolitan ones [GENDER-I5]; a credit-to-deposit ratio of 43 against 93 for men. Microfinance loans fell 27.1 per cent in Karnataka and 23.7 in Tamil Nadu against 21.1 in Bihar [INC-I14]; Andhra Pradesh and Telangana expanded after "a favourable court ruling" [GENDER-I10]; agriculture and animal husbandry are the main purposes of microfinance loans, 79 per cent of them rural joint-liability lending [C4-MEASUREMENT4]; the RBI puts operative Kisan Credit Cards down 11.7 per cent in 2025-26 "primarily on account of select private sector banks reclassifying/declassifying certain exposures" while farm credit grew 15.7 per cent [RBI18]; durable lending across all lenders grew 36.6 per cent [INC-I15]; the bureau counts about 16 crore credit-active women, rising [GENDER-G19].

**Verdict: contested, high confidence.** One count is wrong: the processor drops any district row without a June 2026 value, so Ramanagara's 23.1 lakh loans to women leave Karnataka's March 2024 base while Bengaluru South's 9.0 lakh stay in its end figure; on every row Karnataka fell 20.3 per cent, Rajasthan 15.6 (shown as 5.8) and the comparable base is about 11.4 crore [C4-MEASUREMENT11], so the south did not go the other way. The December 2024 entry does not touch the village and big-city figures: small finance banks' rural and metropolitan small accounts show no step across that quarter (45.1 to 44.7 lakh and 43.0 to 41.4 lakh), the book sits wholly in the urban group, whose loss since December 2024 the story does not quote and which is 0.21 crore as published and about 0.5 crore net of the entry [GAP1-18]. Farm loans: rural small accounts fell 1.17 crore at private banks and 0.31 at public-sector banks; rural accounts of all sizes fell 1.02 crore, all at private and small finance banks, while public-sector banks' rose with rural farm credit up 26 per cent; private banks' rural farm loans average about ₹35,000, a microfinance ticket, and their farm accounts fell 30 per cent while their farm credit rose 7 per cent. That these are joint-liability group loans is the panel's inference from the balances and from the fall of trade, industry and transport accounts in step with agriculture; no bank's disclosure identifies them, and the nearest published support is Sa-Dhan's finding that agriculture and animal husbandry are the main purposes of microfinance loans [C4-MEASUREMENT4]; within the small finance bank group, agriculture accounts fell from 134.6 to 93.8 lakh between September 2024 and June 2026 while personal loans rose [GAP1-17]. The villages' "farm loans" and the east's "women's loans" are largely the same private-bank group loans in two tables. Durables: durable accounts fell from 2.58 to 1.18 crore while the amount on them rose from ₹39,547 to ₹47,232 crore; the metropolitan small accounts fell from 2.14 to 0.70 crore with outstanding only from ₹13,027 to ₹12,700 crore, 0.83 crore of it in the June 2026 quarter, all in Maharashtra and at private banks: a lender purging low-balance accounts while the market grew. Women: men's accounts rose in every state where women's fell, the money lent to women rose in each (Bihar plus 10 per cent, Odisha plus 40), nationally from ₹17.8 to ₹24.6 lakh crore; Maharashtra's fall is Mumbai's re-booking (men's accounts fell there too, Thane gained); the districts that lost most are booking hubs, Vaishali carrying thirteen loans to women per loan to a man at ₹27,000 an account. Assam is outside the comparison, its March 2024 base being zero in the processor, and that is right for the wrong reason: the north-east is in the table from 2014 (B4), but Kamrup Metropolitan's loans to women rose from 2.67 lakh in September 2024 to 15.06 lakh in June 2026 on the entry [GAP1-20], so Assam must stay out, and be said to be out for that reason, once the processor joins the two blocks; the state ranking and the 359 of 643 districts are clean.

**Change.** Fix the processor's state sums and regenerate the women's figures; retitle where-they-went and rewrite both paragraphs; rewrite whose-loans' first paragraph and add the booking-hub reading to its second; correct the women's map note, the so-what's WHAT CHANGED and the method's "like with like" and "where a loan is counted"; keep Assam out of the women's comparison and say why.

## C5 Ten districts get ₹42 of every ₹100

**The literature.** Spatial credit is published on two bases and the practice is to name the basis and normalise by the region's size: BIS [GEO-G16]; the US regulators, who warn a loan "may be recorded at the borrower's headquarters" and set loan shares beside population shares [GEO-G15]; Guiso, Sapienza and Zingales, who measure access rather than volume because large firms borrow outside their region; Kendall's credit to district output; a Gini tracked over years. Small loans are local. The RBI's notes list which tables are by sanction and which by utilisation [GEO-G13]; SBI Research puts the top ten at 49 per cent on the branch basis [GEO-G19]; the RBI's FI-Index gives a district Gini of 0.72 for credit against 0.43 for accounts; state gaps are persistent and partly demand-driven; Bihar is India's largest microfinance state, mostly outside the return [INC-I14].

**Verdict: supported for the money share, contested for the loan counts and the state comparison, high confidence.** The shares recompute exactly and match annual Table 1.3's "credit utilised" column; by sanction (Table 2.14) the top ten hold 48.1 per cent and Mumbai's two districts 20.9 against 16.5, so the basis moves the top by six points and the bottom not at all; the top-ten share was 57.9 per cent in 2010; the populations match the Registrar General's projections within half a per cent. But "get" reads utilisation as deployment when the return records the borrower's office: New Delhi district, with 1.4 lakh residents at the 2011 census on its old boundary and 7.7 lakh on the current one, is shown using ₹12.4 lakh crore, and the ten districts held under 5 per cent of India's people in 2011 (Census 2011 figures as transcribed on Wikipedia and City Population [C5-EVIDENCE5, C5-MEASUREMENT3, C5-ALTERNATIVE-4], not checked against the Census tables). "Only 23% of the loans" is where card portfolios are booked: Mumbai Suburban's 3.38 crore accounts are 94 per cent credit cards, the ten districts hold 55 per cent of India's card accounts but 12.9 per cent of other loans, and Mumbai's two districts carry 5.14 crore accounts for 1.24 crore residents. "Mumbai" is one district in one sentence and two in the next. The state paragraph mixes bases and coverage: 81 and ₹4.6 lakh are from quarterly Table 1.3, branch-based and without regional rural banks, which hold 17.6 per cent of Bihar's accounts; like for like, Maharashtra has 68 loans per hundred people and Bihar 12, and without cards 32 against 11. And the state map is contaminated in one state: Assam's 140.0 lakh accounts in March 2026 include about 0.9 crore of the merged bank's book reported from Guwahati, Assam having had 47.5 lakh accounts in September 2024 and 118.9 lakh in December [GAP1-19], so its loans per hundred people are about three times what its own borrowers would give; the Maharashtra and Bihar comparison is unaffected.

**Change.** "Get" becomes "hold"; the paragraph names the basis, distinguishes Mumbai city from Mumbai with its suburbs, gives the sanction-basis share and the card booking; the state paragraph names basis and coverage and gives the like-for-like figures; both figure notes name basis, coverage and, for Assam, the booked accounts, which the map must net out or footnote.

## B1 Banks only

The story never says that NBFCs, microfinance companies, lending apps and cooperatives are outside the returns. The literature states the perimeter under every figure [GEO-G15] and tests substitution with all-lender data: US bank small-business lending 24 per cent below its pre-crisis level while total lending did not vary with bank dependence [SUB-G3]; shadow banks partially filling regulatory gaps; a supervisory tightening followed by non-banks' share rising by half; substitution partial, slow and dearer. In India banks hold 25.3 per cent of a ₹3.29 lakh crore microfinance book [TL22] and 312 of 1,041 lakh accounts, with 57 of Sa-Dhan's 118 members lending as banks' agents on bank books [SUB-I17]; digital NBFCs held 45 per cent of active personal loans and sanctioned 13.2 crore in 2025-26 [SUB-I10]; fintechs made 89 per cent of loans under ₹50,000; NBFCs write 85 to 88 per cent of durable originations [INC-I15]; bank credit to NBFCs grew 26.3 per cent in 2025-26 on the Annual Report's measure [RBI18], after 7.4 per cent in 2024-25, the year the surcharge applied, on the sectoral deployment series, which gives 26.6 for 2025-26 [SUB-I26, not opened; read from the repository export]. One crossing of the perimeter is measured: when Slice's NBFC book was amalgamated into a small finance bank, about 0.7 crore loan accounts and roughly ₹2,100 crore of net advances, about ₹3,000 an account, entered the return in a single quarter [GAP1-1, GAP1-16], 2.6 per cent of all small borrowal accounts, and movements the other way, bank small loans replaced by NBFC or microfinance lending, are invisible in the same tables. **Verdict: supported with caveats, medium confidence, the size referee having given medium.** The referees put 27 to 36 crore accounts of the story's size outside the return against 23.1 crore inside it. Microfinance shrank at every lender type (banks 441 to 311 lakh accounts, NBFC-MFIs 526 to 435, small finance banks 210 to 160 [SUB-I17]), so the bank fall understates that retreat; durable and personal loans grew outside banks, so the bank fall overstates that one; net, formal small-loan accounts fell by roughly 2 to 3.5 crore, not 4.2. **Change:** a "Banks only" paragraph in the method note, with the October 2024 entry as the yardstick of how far one lender's status can move the count; "loans in the country" becomes "bank loans"; seriesFall and where-they-went scope the fall to banks and say where the market grew; "access to credit" becomes "access to bank credit".

## B2 The ₹2 lakh line is fixed in nominal rupees

The cut-off was ₹10,000 from 1972, ₹25,000 from 1984 and ₹2 lakh from the March 1999 return, and Chavan states the caveat: "there is a methodological issue in comparing SBAs across time given the changes in price levels" [PRICE-I3]. The FDIC found an unadjusted loan-size line misdated the US recovery by two to four years [DEF-G5]; GAO, the SBA and the EU re-base such lines at least every five years [DEF-G4]; Ramakumar and Chavan show Indian size classes draining over decades [INC-I5]. Every neighbouring policy line has moved: the collateral-free farm limit to exactly ₹2 lakh from January 2025; KCC limits stepped up 10 per cent a year [C3-MEASUREMENT3]; the interest-subvention ceiling at ₹3 lakh for 2025-26 [B2-WORDING4], which the Budget of 1 February 2025 proposed raising to ₹5 lakh, a rise not yet in the scheme circular [B2-SIZE6, B2-WORDING5]; MFIN's own ₹2 lakh cap [TL6]. **Verdict: supported with caveats, high confidence.** By the CPI-IW, ₹2 lakh of March 1999 is about ₹9.8 lakh in June 2026 [DEF-G16 gives ₹9.5 lakh]: the June 2026 index of 151.9 on the 2016 base is the Labour Bureau's [DEF-I12], while the March 1999 index of 414 on the 1982 base and the Bureau's linking factors of 4.63 and 2.88 were read from the Bureau's series in the full record and are not carried by the release cited. A limit is fixed at sanction, so loans cross only at renewal or replacement. Over the eighteen months the drift is small: accounts above the line grew 2.24 crore in the previous eighteen months and 2.34 after, the excess about 0.4 crore; prices rose 5.7 per cent against an average limit of ₹82,945 in the ₹25,000 to 2 lakh band; loans of every size fell 1.85 crore; the referees' estimate is 0.2 to 0.45 crore of excess drift, at most 1.0 crore. Durables and microfinance loans (₹27,000 to ₹62,000) cannot cross. Farm loans in the south did: small farm accounts fell 1.59 crore and above-line farm accounts rose 0.65 crore in six southern states against 4 lakh in the east. That limits there rose with the price of gold is the referees' inference from the average limit of the remaining small farm accounts in Tamil Nadu rising from ₹91,820 to ₹1,05,150 and from DBIE's gold-price series, with the Annual Report recording the growth in gold loans [RBI18]; part of the farm fall is private banks declassifying KCC exposures [RBI18]. **Change:** seriesFall gives the bound; the method note dates the line, prices it and states the mechanism.

## B3 Accounts are not people

The story never says "borrowers", but draws three conclusions about people from counts of accounts: "more Indians can borrow", "those who borrow the least", and a chapter titled "Whose loans closed". The standard is one borrower per person however many accounts [INC-G15]; loan-level and borrower-level effects diverge; crisis markets show 21 to 40 per cent of borrowers with several lenders [MFI-G6] and two contracts per borrower in Bosnia. Indian bureaus give the conversions: 2.06 cards per holder [INC-I17]; 1.54 to 1.56 microfinance loans per borrower, 1.72 to 1.77 in Bihar, Jharkhand and Odisha [SUB-I17]; 19.2 per cent of bank microfinance borrowers with more than one loan from the same lender; 25 crore credit-active adults [INC-I16]; 16 crore formal borrowers in a year [INC-G13]; about 16 crore credit-active women, rising [GENDER-G19]. The merged bank shows the gap from the other side: it reports "20 million+" registered customers and more than 5 million savings accounts and publishes no count of loan accounts or borrowers [GAP1-2, GAP1-9, GAP1-11]; the only count of its loan accounts is the BSR step itself, about 0.7 crore at entry and about a crore by June 2026 [GAP1-16]. **Verdict: supported with caveats, high confidence.** Cards and microfinance are 45 per cent of the 26.24 crore small accounts, and the referees put the pile at roughly 16 to 20 crore people. Accounts fell about twice as fast as people wherever people are counted: 352 lakh microfinance accounts closed for 102 lakh borrowers lost between December 2024 and March 2026 [SUB-I17]; minus 22 against minus 17 per cent at CRIF [INC-I14]; the guardrails worked by cutting lenders per borrower. So 4.2 crore fewer accounts are roughly 1.5 to 3 crore fewer people with a bank loan. The fall in women's accounts awaits regeneration on the corrected base (C4); on the same ratios it will be roughly half as large in women as in accounts, while the money on women's accounts rose 38 per cent and the bureau's count of women with credit rose. "Nearly two deposit accounts for every person" is 2.9 per adult with an account [INC-G13]. **Change:** the so-what gets the unit and the bureau's person count; "those who borrow the least" becomes "the smallest loans"; whose-loans says accounts in women's names and gives the ratio; the method note carries the ratios; wherever a counting sentence says "small loans", the proposed text says "loan accounts".

## B4 Where a loan is counted

The RBI's notes list Tables 1.3, 1.5, 1.16, 1.17 and 2.1 to 2.4 as by place of sanction and Tables 1.10, 1.11 and others by utilisation [GEO-G13]; the international practice is two bases, named [GEO-G16]; Ramakumar and Chavan showed city branches booking a third of farm credit [INC-I5]; MFIN records banks reclassifying microfinance to retail [TL22]. **Verdict: supported with caveats, high confidence, and wider than the item lists.** The method note has the basis wrong twice: the quarterly Tables 2.1 and 3.5a behind the village-to-city losses are sanction-basis (the quarterly metropolitan count, 14.23 crore, matches the sanction figure of 14.27 in annual Table 1.2, not the utilisation figure of 13.18), and the groups scene's "where the bank branch is" is wrong for loans, which the Handbook counts by use [DEF-I14]. ₹8.31 lakh crore sanctioned in Maharashtra is used in other states; Mumbai city has 4.41 crore accounts by sanction and 1.76 by use. Mumbai's change is measurable: between March and June 2026 loans to individuals booked in Mumbai Suburban fell from ₹9.44 to ₹3.76 lakh crore, men's and women's alike, while the all-India total rose; the two districts lost 18.1 lakh loans to women in that quarter while the rest of India gained 3.4 lakh. The Guwahati entry shows the mechanism in the other direction: a nationwide digital book booked at the sanctioning office in Guwahati appears as urban Assam in Table 2.1, the population group of the reporting office, as Assam in Table 1.3 and as Kamrup Metropolitan in Table 1.10, with the December 2024 steps agreeing within 2 lakh across the three [GAP1-18, GAP1-19, GAP1-20]; the bank's identity in the RBI's schedule is continuous through its renaming in May 2025, so the book reports under one bank from December 2024 on [GAP1-13], its registered office and 105 of its 159 branches are in Assam [GAP1-2], and Kamrup Metropolitan, which carried 12.6 lakh loan accounts of individuals in September 2024, carried 111.5 lakh by June 2026 [GAP1-20]. The north-east premise is false: DBIE lists the seven states twice, under "NORTH-EASTERN REGION" to June 2025 and "NORTH EASTERN REGION" from September 2025, and the processor drops the earlier block; joined, their loans to women rose from 24.9 to 34.7 lakh, Assam from 16.6 to 26.9 lakh, but Assam's rise is the entry (Kamrup Metropolitan's loans to women went from 2.67 to 15.06 lakh [GAP1-20]), so Assam stays out of the women's comparison for that reason while the other six can come in. Regional rural banks hold 8.5 per cent of small accounts but 15.8 per cent of their money, a fifth of small accounts in Bihar and Uttar Pradesh and 1.3 per cent in Maharashtra, and 18.7 per cent of women's loans in Andhra Pradesh. Reclassification to retail moves accounts between occupation heads only. **Change:** the method note's "Where a loan is counted" states which figures are on which basis, sizes the gap, states the Mumbai re-booking as measured and names the Guwahati entry beside it; "Like with like" says state totals count every row, that the north-east is in the table from 2014, and why Assam is still left out; the groups scene and the deposits figure say deposits by branch, loans by use; seriesStart sizes the regional rural bank gap.

## Best practice for reading credit data, and how the story measures up

1. Report accounts and money as two named measures: the story has both but fuses them in its so-what. Partly followed.
2. Say "accounts" and put a person-level count beside it: the method note does, the conclusions do not. Partly followed.
3. State the lender perimeter and check the lenders outside it before calling a fall a loss: not followed.
4. Date a fixed nominal line, price it and bound the drift with the bands: conceded, neither dated nor bounded. Partly followed.
5. Mark every perimeter break and never splice across one: the two-sets rule is right; the 2017 and 2024 entries are unmarked. Partly followed.
6. Attribute a change to a policy with a comparison group and report its withdrawal: not followed.
7. Check reclassification and re-booking before reading a fall as closure, and check entries as well as exits: applied to Mumbai only. Partly followed.
8. Name the counting basis under every spatial figure and quantify the gap: named once, and wrong for the village-to-city figures. Partly followed.
9. Normalise spatial credit by population or output and report a concentration index over time: not followed.
10. Decompose a rate, label capped rates as floors, separate products: the processor knows the rate is a slope indicator, the page calls it an average. Partly followed.
11. Judge a small-loan rate against the borrower's outside option: not followed.
12. Pair every count with the rupee figure for the same period: partly followed.
13. Set the supply-side count beside a demand-side survey: not followed.
14. For gender, name the product first, separate demand from supply, use shares: not followed.
15. Compute every figure by program with checks: followed; the one error was in row coverage, which the checks do not test.
